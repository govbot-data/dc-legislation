Dear Chairman Mendelson:
Enclosed for consideration by the Council are the proposed “Robert F. Kennedy Campus Master Plan
Approval Resolution of 2026” and the accompanying master plan.
The Robert F. Kennedy Campus Master Plan serves as a framework for developing 180 acres of land
along the Anacostia River into a vibrant, inclusive, and resilient year-round destination. The campus will
connect communities on both sides of the river, expand economic opportunity, and celebrate the cultural
and recreational life of DC. The master plan will be used to guide site development, coordinate priorities
with federal stakeholders, and provide the business community with guidelines for development and
infrastructure improvements.
This master plan is rooted in input from neighborhoods, small businesses, community organizations, and
other stakeholders, including 3,000 written comments received during the public comment period. The
resulting plan contains a development framework to guide the buildout of six unique districts across the
site that include 5,500 to 6,500 housing units, new mobility networks, and a collection of connected parks
and open spaces.
I pleased to take another step forward towards delivering on this transformational opportunity that will
drive growth for years to come. I encourage the Council’s prompt and favorable enactment of this
legislation.
Sincerely,
Muriel Bowser
Mayor

September 24, 2026
Dear District of Columbia Councilmembers:
This letter contains the executive summary of changes from the public review draft to the final draft
of the Robert F. Kennedy Campus Master Plan (“RFK Campus Master Plan”), as required by DC
Code Section 1-306.03(c)(4).
On June 30, 2026, the Office of Planning (“OP”) released the draft master plan for a 45-day public
comment period. Notification about the availability of the draft, the date of the Mayoral Hearing,
and dates for the public comment period was provided in the District of Columbia Register, to the
project e-mail list, OP social media, OurRFK.dc.gov website, Advisory Neighborhood
Commissioners, community-based organizations, postcard mailers to households within a half-
mile, and through door-to-door canvassing. The draft was posted online availability of the master
plan, and printed copies were made available at public facilities including the Martin Luther King Jr.
Memorial Library, Rosedale and Benning/Dorothy I. Height Libraries, St. Coletta of Greater
Washington, Marshall Heights Community Development Organization offices, and the FH
Faunteroy Center & Resilience Incubator.
During the public comment period, which closed on August 14, 2026, OP received over 3,100
comments from more than 1,400 stakeholders. OP received comments primarily via email and
through an online form accessible from the project website during the public comment period. A
mayoral public hearing, hosted by OP, was held on August 1, 2026, at St. Coletta of Greater
Washington at 1901 Independence Ave SE, to provide the opportunity to comment on the draft
master plan in-person. At the public hearing, 37 people presented oral testimony, and 17 people
who were unable to stay for the duration of the hearing submitted oral testimony via a dedicated
project voicemail. Four community office hours sessions at Rosedale and Benning/Dorothy I.
Height Libraries (on 7/15, 7/25, 7,29 and 8/8) and five pop-up stations at community events in the
surrounding neighborhoods offered opportunities to answer questions and describe ways to
submit written comments.
The redevelopment of the RFK Stadium Campus is a high-profile initiative, and the comments on
the draft master plan covered a broad range of topics and perspectives. Overall, the comments
suggest that DC residents are excited about the campus’s redevelopment. Many commenters
advocated specific amenities, design features, and programs for the site. But several common
themes emerged. These themes and how the master plan addresses them are described below:
Page 1

Density and Architectural Character
Many commenters expressed excitement about the proposed building heights and mixed-use
development program, emphasizing the importance of maximizing housing production. A similar
number of commenters, mostly neighboring residents from Kingman Park and Hill East, expressed
concern about building heights adjacent to existing low-rise residential neighborhoods and modern
architecture that might feel out of character.
The RFK Campus Master Plan takes a balanced approach to building density and architecture.
While the development program generally calls for high-density mixed-use buildings in the
Riverfront, Plaza, and Kingman Commons Districts, the zoning framework and design guidelines
identify locations for lower building heights and context-sensitive architecture. Some strategies
include the use of building height transition zones along existing neighborhoods (pages 92-93 and
118-121), contextually appropriate building materials (pages 122-125), and facade articulations
(pages 116-117).
Housing Affordability
Commenters expressed excitement about the amount of new housing proposed. However, many
raised concerns that requirements for affordability were not clearly communicated and questioned
how affordable housing would be delivered and whether it would be sufficient. Commenters were
also concerned about rising property values, that amenities would serve new residents and
visitors, and that the redevelopment could spur displacement in surrounding neighborhoods.
The final master plan continues to use the affordability thresholds laid out in D.C. Act 26-150,
which requires more robust than levels of affordability than other affordability policies, like
Inclusionary Zoning or Planned Unit Developments. Additionally, the final master plan adds new
language to clarify the low and very low-income requirements from the Council legislation (pages
28-29) as well as expectations for the equitable distribution and delivery of affordable housing
across the entire campus (page 98). The master plan identifies several existing District policies and
strategies for displacement prevention. These include property tax relief, home purchase
assistance, and tenant protection programs.
Environmental Sustainability & Anacostia River Protection
Comments on environmental sustainability and river protection focused on ensuring the site’s
long-term ecological health and strengthening the Anacostia River as a natural resource. Many
Page 2

commenters expressed strong support for the master plan’s sustainability principles and asked for
additional detail about implementation strategies.
The final master plan contains lighting standards to reduce light pollution (pages 182-183),
specifies how tree canopy expansion aligns with DDOT guidance (page 140), and highlights DOEE’s
existing work on river restoration (page 175). Some requests, such as mandates on single-use
plastics or net-zero building energy standards, fall outside the scope of the master plan but may be
considered in future policy or development discussions. Detailed analysis of environmental
impacts from new development and mitigation measures are expected in the findings of the
Environmental Impact Statement, in compliance with the National Environmental Policy Act,
anticipated for release in Spring 2027.
Public Transit and Transportation Systems
Feedback on transportation systems reflected broad interest in safe pedestrian and bicycle
connections, reliable transit access, and mitigation of traffic impacts. Many commenters
encouraged stronger commitments to pedestrian-first design and more detail on bike network
expansion. Residents and stakeholders in neighborhoods east of the Anacostia River, including
River Terrace and Mayfair among others, emphasized long-standing connectivity gaps between
neighborhoods east of the Anacostia River and the RFK Campus.
In response, the final master plan adds new text to better describe the street grid and safety
improvements (page 58), strengthens descriptions of regional bike network integration (pages 60-
61), and adds a new section addressing connectivity with surrounding neighborhoods (pages 62-
63). The plan also includes updated guidance for traffic operations and parking management to
address everyday and event-day circulation (pages 101).
Many commenters also weighed in on the proposed public transit for the site by expressing a desire
for a more “transit-first” approach. This included questions about the Gold Line and continued
request for a new infill Metro Rail station on Oklahoma Avenue. The final draft includes a
substantially expanded narrative about the proposed transit planning (pages 60 and 63), which was
not yet available at the time of publication of the draft master plan. While the final master plan
does not propose a new infill station, due to WMATA’s conclusion that such a station is infeasible
due to cost, engineering, and timeline constraints, it does not preclude future consideration of an
infill station.
Other transportation comments focused on traffic and parking impacts. A group of commenters
acknowledged that redevelopment will result in new traffic patterns and requested assurances of
mitigation strategies. Many expressed concerns about event-day traffic caused by an influx of
Page 3

visitors, daily traffic that could result from more residents, and reduced pedestrian access due to
the width of the newly proposed road connecting Benning Road NE and C Street NE.
In addition to the changes previously mentioned, the final master plan includes a new section on
“Traffic Operations and Parking Plan” that would be implemented before the opening of the
stadium and include neighborhoods beyond the master plan boundary (page 101). Detailed
analysis of traffic impacts from new development and mitigation measures are expected in the
Environmental Impact Statement (page 97). The final master plan also adds a general cross section
of the new roadway, identified as KC-A (page 130). However, DDOT will refine the roadway design
to enhance pedestrian access and safety
Parking Garage Design, Scale & Placement
Comments on structured parking reflected multiple perspectives on balancing mobility needs with
environmental, neighborhood, and design considerations. Stakeholders raised questions about
ensuring sufficient stadium event parking and minimizing neighborhood spillover, reducing visual
impacts on adjacent residential areas, and enabling long-term adaptive reuse of the parking
structures. Other stakeholders suggested finding alternative placement of the parking structures,
particularly the G2 garage in the Kingman Commons.
The final master plan enhances guidance on exterior design standards for the parking structures
(pages 125 and 129-134) and adds expectations for non-event day activation and temporary uses
(pages 79, 80, and 101-102). The location of the two parking structures has been informed by
traffic circulation assessments, and their placement near existing infrastructure such as the metro
viaduct and Independence Avenue allows for buffering from existing residences through new
development and reduces cut-through traffic in the surrounding neighborhoods. Finally, the design
of the parking garages is now underway, and garage screening and architectural integration with
the surrounding area are being studied.
For new parking incorporated into mixed-use building development, new text provides specific
zoning guidance to consider fully eliminating or reducing parking minimums and encourages all on-
site parking to be below-grade (pages 90 and 101).
Neighborhood-Serving Amenities
Comments on neighborhood-serving amenities highlighted broad enthusiasm for everyday
services, including food retail, healthcare services, and community-oriented businesses. Many
Page 4

commenters encouraged additional detail on commitments and phasing to ensure that recreation
and transportation amenities support residents throughout the development process.
In response, the final master plan expands language on grocery and food retail (pages 75, 79, and
100), strengthens implementation guidance for attracting essential services, such as pharmacies
and daycare providers (page 100), and provides new detail on interim uses to support activation
during early phases (page 102). Requests from interested small businesses were shared with
District agencies who will be tasked with implementation of CBE requirements as part of site
redevelopment.
Recreation
Comments on recreation generally expressed strong support for preserving The Fields, building the
Sportsplex, and expanding site-wide recreational offerings. Requests often focused on specific
sports, such as tournament-level pickleball and pole vaulting, or facilities, such as a circus arts
school, which are typically addressed through later operations and facilities planning processes.
The final master plan therefore clarifies opportunities for interim recreational uses (page 102) and
updates conceptual renderings for certain facilities, such as pickleball courts (page 142).
Implementation
Comments emphasized the importance of clear governance, accountability, and consistent
implementation over time. To address these concerns, the final master plan expands its
‘Implementation’ section (pages 96-103), including new language on governance structures, site
maintenance responsibilities, and interagency coordination (page 102). The Role of the Master Plan
and Master Plan Area sections were re-arranged for clarity (pages 12-13), and stronger language
was added to set expectations for compliance with the master plan by private developers,
including the Washington Commanders.
Many of these comments sought commitments for funding and infrastructure investment that are
beyond the scope of a master plan. Some, such as the funding and completion of Phase 2 of the
Benning Road Improvement Project, the funding and construction of the Gold Line, including Phase
2 out to the Benning Road Metro Rail station, and the creation of improved bike and pedestrian
facilities on the Whitney Young Memorial Bridge, are more appropriately addressed through the
Capital Improvement Plan process. Others suggested policy strategies, such as incentives to
attract a grocery store or community meeting rooms within new development. And still others
relied on future planning and implementation processes, such as the eventual creation of a Traffic
Page 5

Operations and Parking Plan, the completion of the Kingman and Heritage Islands Development
Project, or the specific programming of the Sportsplex or new park spaces.
Changes in Response to Changing Circumstances
Since the publication of the draft master plan on June 30, 2026, a few external circumstances were
brought to the attention of the project team that needed to be reflected in the master plan.
First, the District entered negotiations with the federal government for a jurisdictional transfer of
the DC Armory, which is being vacated by the National Guard. The final master plan includes two
new pages that expand on the opportunities provided by that site (pages 86-87). Additionally, the
new information about the location of some utility lines required the re-configuration of Kingman
Commons (page 79), and new information about the siting needs for a transit plaza to support the
Gold Line initiated additional language around buffering any new transit plaza from view from the
Kingman Park neighborhood (pages 63, 80, and 108).

Conclusion
Funds are sufficient in the revised fiscal year 2026 budget and the fiscal year 2027 through fiscal year
2030 budget and financial plan to implement the resolution.
Background
The resolution approves the Robert F. Kennedy Campus Master Plan (‘Plan”), which provides
guidance on the development of the area that was the previous site of RFK Stadium. The campus is in
Ward 7 and is bounded by Benning Road NE to the north; Oklahoma Avenue N.E., C Street N.E., and
19th Street N.E. and S.E. to the west; Independence Avenue S.E. and Water Street S.E. to the south;
and the Anacostia River to the east.
The Office of Planning initiated the development of the Plan in March 2026, and the draft plan was
made public on June 30, 2026.1 The Plan provides supplemental guidance on the campus
development to the Zoning Commission and other District agencies carrying out the policies of the
Comprehensive Plan for the National Capital: District Elements.2

Funds are sufficient in the revised fiscal year 2026 budget and the fiscal year 2027 through fiscal year

commit District resources to implement the recommendations.

Development across the RFK Campus will create new demands for on-site utility capacity. The
master plan primarily focuses on land uses and their intersections with design, transportation,
and broader economic development. As such, the master plan recommends high-level
sustainability principles to guide the development of new utility infrastructure and energy
standards (page 141) and references potential future needs for utilities later in the
development process (page 101). Technical details about which parties bear the costs of new
utility demand fall outside of the scope of the RFK Campus Master Plan and purview of the
Office of Planning.

has retained an Energy Master Planner who will assess how the site meets future energy needs.
The resulting plan will outline a modern, campus-wide system that uses a mix of on-site
resources, such as solar energy and networked heating and cooling infrastructure, to make the
Stadium and surrounding development more efficient, reliable, and cost-effective to operate.
Together, these features aim to reduce long-term energy costs, improve resilience for the site
and community, and support a cleaner low-carbon campus.

A Note on Historical References
This draft cites material from the ANC 6A website archive to illustrate institutional memory
from the past two decades. Where used, it is clearly marked below in “Historical context”
call-outs and is included only to point OP/DDOT/DMPED back to past ANC infrastructure
priorities. It is not offered as, and should not be read as, the Commission's current position on
land use limitations.
This matters here because the Commission's own outlook may have changed substantially over
time. Today's ANC 6A is especially supportive of housing growth, urban density, and
transit-oriented development. We are focused on building a campus—and a city—that works for
residents across a range of ages, abilities, socioeconomic, and other groups, building on lessons
from the 8-to-80 Cities movement.3
1. Overarching Comment: Require Binding Commitments, Timelines, and Unified
Governance
Our strongest and most consistent piece of feedback is that while the master plan presents an
aspirational and attractive multi-generational vision, it remains largely speculative. Aside from
the stadium itself and immediate support operations, the plan lacks concrete timelines, funding

Specific gaps we demand OP address in the next phase:

● Unified Maintenance and Security Entity: The current proposed "hodgepodge" split

maintenance, safety, and security of public spaces, Kingman Island, and the Anacostia

● Enforceable Sustainability and Amenities: Speculative mentions of grocery stores,

left as passive suggestions.

2. Housing, Density, and Multi-Generational Community
Consistent with our updated vision, the plan must maximize residential density and ensure
neighborhood-building is structurally prioritized over purely event-day entertainment.
Specific neighborhood requirements:

within the immediate 0.5-mile walkshed of the Stadium-Armory Metro station.
● Sequence Safeguards and Penalties: There must be strict regulatory and contractual

neighborhood. The stadium complex must not be permitted to outpace the delivery of
housing, adequate non-automobile transportation infrastructure, and local retail.

account for a market-rate, multi-level continuum of care facility (independent living,

assisted living, and skilled nursing). Currently, our aging neighbors are forced to leave

aspirational targets—so the neighborhood remains attainable for existing Ward 6, 7, and 8

3. Transportation, Access, and Parking: Prioritize People Over Cars
Claims that this will be a "transit-first" development will continue to ring hollow so long as

construction, particularly as the District has already recorded 27 traffic deaths in 2026 through

● Acknowledge and Protect Existing Wins: We commend the master plan for preserving
the 19th Street NE protected bike lane — a facility that could easily have been sacrificed
to campus redesign — and for beginning to establish a protected bike facility along East
Capitol Street. These are exactly the kind of concrete, non-negotiable commitments we
are asking the plan to extend campus-wide.
● Extend Protected Bike Infrastructure Beyond the Campus Boundary: The master

Strategic Bike Plan letter4 to DDOT for continuous, protected bike infrastructure
connecting Union Station to the RFK campus along the East Capitol Street,
Massachusetts Avenue, 4th Street, and 6th Street corridor, which we expect to become the
primary route for micromobility users traveling from Metro and the H Street corridor to
the campus.
● Ground Bike Infrastructure Demands in Existing District Policy: The District's own
moveDC 2021 plan already commits to fully protected bikeways on East Capitol Street,
Benning Road NE, and Pennsylvania Avenue SE — commitments DDOT has since

Safety and Mobility Improvements project design. The RFK master plan must not repeat

this commitment extend into Wards 7 and 8 — connecting Mayfair, Fort Dupont, Capitol

The parking structure should be reduced in size and the park (p. 64 item 9) should be

lessons regarding injuries, impaired accessibility, high maintenance costs, and liability
associated with brick pedestrian pathways. While porous materials are essential for
managing stormwater runoff, the design standards should specify durable, flat, modern
porous compounds instead of standard brick or cobblestone.

amenities program should include an Olympic competition-sized swimming pool and an
indoor track facility sized to support sanctioned DC-based athletic competitions. Nearby
Eastern High School has no pool of its own, and DC-based indoor track competitions
currently must be held at the PG County Sports and Learning Complex, forcing DC
families to leave the District just to watch their own scholar-athletes compete. A

event-day visitors.

5. Environmental Review, Stormwater Management, and Climate Resilience
The campus sits directly along the Anacostia River and adjacent to Kingman Lake and Kingman
Island, and the scale of the redevelopment warrants a rigorous, enforceable environmental review
and management framework—both during construction and over the life of the campus.
Environmental and climate requirements:
● Commission a Full Environmental Impact Statement: Given the site's size,
environmental sensitivity, and riverfront location, the District should commission a full
Environmental Impact Statement (EIS) for the campus rather than relying on a more
limited review.
● Strict Compliance with Stormwater and Soil Laws: All applicable District laws and
regulations governing stormwater runoff and retention, and toxic or contaminated soil
removal and remediation, must be strictly followed and independently verified
throughout every phase of redevelopment.
● Gold-Standard Construction-Phase Management: The District must require and
actively maintain gold-standard stormwater, sediment, and litter management practices
during all demolition and construction activities, not only after buildings are occupied.
● Future-Sized Green Infrastructure: Bioretention and other green stormwater
infrastructure for the completed campus must comply with all District laws and be sized
for the rain intensity expected at the end of the stadium's assumed useful life, not
present-day rainfall patterns.
● Wetlands Restoration for Sea-Level Rise: The plan should restore wetlands in
Kingman Lake and in upland areas expected to become wetlands by the end of the
stadium's assumed life, given sea-level rise currently conservatively projected at 3.4 feet
by 2080.
● Comprehensive Litter Management: The plan should prioritize reusable containers
over single-use plastics and build out recycling infrastructure across the stadium and
campus, and install trash traps—such as Bandalong or Bandit litter traps—in open-air
stormwater conveyances before they discharge into Kingman Lake. The stadium should
be required to allow visitors to bring in one small plastic water bottle per person, aligned
with regulations at Nats Park,5 to reduce waste.
● Protect Kingman Island: Redevelopment activity must be designed and managed to
avoid and mitigate environmental impacts on Kingman Island's natural areas, which
should be treated as an asset to protect, not a buffer to build around.

opening is the single biggest economic opportunity for H Street in years, but only if
game-day and event traffic is designed to route people through the corridor — shopping,
eating, and stopping — rather than around it via shuttles that skip directly to the stadium.
● Make Shared Parking Commitments Binding, Not Aspirational: The master plan
already notes that the two committed parking garages "should be considered for potential
shared parking options" to serve special events, retail, and other daily users — but this

8. Connect RFK to H Street's Revitalization, Not Apart From It

RFK-driven ridership on both event and non-event days.

or densities that are unlikely to be realized in reasonable time.

and host the Campus must be a “north star” guiding principle in planning and construction and development of the

5. The RFK Campus Requires a World-Class Transit-First Infrastructure
Returning to our views on a transit-first approach, we believe it is indisputable that a successful RFK MP Draft should
include planning for and provision of an infill Metrorail station to provide a localized, second means of egress to the
Campus and the communities surrounding it. A “world class” facility demands “world-class” transit infrastructure. This
principle should also extend to improving existing Metrorail infrastructure by relocating the existing train viaduct to an
underground routing to the maximum extent possible. This approach will free up above-ground surface area for
additional campus programming. The opportune moment to relocate this infrastructure to an underground location
would occur before any vertical construction in the area of Parking Garage 2.
We also recognize a “Gold Line” bus rapid transit (BRT) option has been proposed to expedite visitors to the Campus
from Washington Union Station. If this option is implemented, our Commission has already recommended that any
infrastructure for a “bus depot” should not be located at the intersection of 21st and C Streets, NE, but should instead be
located closer to Benning Road, NE, in an area more capable of handling sustained larger vehicle traffic the arrivals and
departures of a large number of visitors.
We further recommend that the planning process meaningfully pursue options to leverage nearby rail infrastructure to
accommodate MARC and VRE commuter rail services to serve the Campus, whether routinely or on “game days.”
We further recommend the planning process study, assess and make recommendations for improvements to the
Anacostia River Trail system, as it is likely to attract substantial new pedestrian and micromobility traffic as the Campus
is built out. The study should also make safety improvement recommendations (e.g., installing low-impact solar
lighting, designating separate pedestrian/bicycle rights of way, etc).
6. A Thoughtful, Dynamic and Enforced Construction Traffic Management Plan Will Be Critical to Maintaining
Quality of Life in the Surrounding Communities
Construction of the many elements of the Campus will occur over multiple years and will require importation of all
materials to the RFK site. This will engender regular heavy truck traffic carrying heavy materials through communities
that connect to the site. We recommend a construction traffic transportation management plan be established early on
to ensure such traffic operates within negotiated hours, using designated routes to minimize traffic and noise
disruptions to neighborhood homes, businesses, schools and other activities. Compliance with the plan should be
regularly monitored and enforced by District agencies. Construction traffic should be routed using the least invasive
means and routes periodically re-evaluated.
7. Effective, Funded Support to Neighborhood Communities Will be Essential to Control Campus Spillovers
Large-scale events are likely to lead to spillover effects accumulating in neighboring communities which should not be
left to residents to control. Instead, planning, funding and personnel should be made available to the community by the
District Government, the Washington Commanders, and EventsDC (or the Campus operating authority) to manage and
mitigate these spillovers. These neighborhood spillovers may include illegal parking, unauthorized tailgating in public
space (such as Kenilworth Park on public streets in proximity to the Campus), trash accumulation in public space, and
concentration of rental bicycles and other micromobility assets.

Dear Secretary Howard,
Today, along with Chairman Phil Mendelson and Councilmembers Charles Allen, Christina
Henderson, Matthew Frumin, Anita Bonds, Elissa Silverman, Brianne Nadeau, and Zachary
Parker, I am introducing the “Supporting Survivors of Domestic Violence and Abuse Amendment
Act of 2026,” which would offer a set of structural supports for survivors and victims affected by
domestic, intimate partner, sexual, and gender-based violence and abuse by fortifying public safety
responses and enshrining survivor-centered protections in law.
Domestic violence denotes “a pattern of abusive behavior in any relationship that is used by one
partner to gain or maintain power and control over another intimate partner, dating partner, or
family member,” which includes “physical, sexual, emotional, economic, or psychological actions
or threats of actions that influence another person.”i In the District, 41% of women and 21% of
men will have suffered physical and sexual violence in a domestic or intimate partner context in
their lifetime.ii An overwhelming 99% of survivors will have suffered economic and financial
abuse.iii During the course of one day (24 hours) in the District in 2025, 103 survivors contacted
domestic violence hotlines and 790 survivors sought assistance from service providers in the
District, including 339 survivors seeking emergency shelter/housing.iv “Survivors at the
margins”—in particular “Black survivors, immigrant and limited-English survivors, LGBTQ+
survivors, survivors with disabilities, youth, and survivors who have been trafficked or
criminalized”—are disproportionately impacted.v Alarmingly, while the rates for homicides
overall have decreased in the last several years, the rates of domestic violence-related homicides
have not.vi
Interventions to reduce violence, increase survivor support, and protect victim privacy are needed.
As the product of close conversation and collaboration with service providers, survivors,
advocates, and community members, this legislation would,
1. Codify and fortify within MPD’s Special Investigative Branch a Domestic Violence
Response Unit to reduce and respond to incidents of domestic violence and intimate
partner violence and other incidents of sexual or gender-based violence as appropriate. The
Unit will consist of at least two Detectives and two Lieutenants dedicated to pursuing
domestic violence warrants; one specialized Domestic Violence Prosecutor; one data

specialist; one Director of Domestic Violence Prevention and Intervention; and one victim
and domestic violence specialist, assigned to liaise with the Victim Services Branch.
2. Clarify and codify gun relinquishment after violation of a civil protection order.
Currently, there is a knowledge and compliance gap when it comes to the enforcement of
gun relinquishment when a firearm-possessing perpetrator violates a civil protection order.
This provision explicitly clarifies what gun relinquishment entails when a civil protection
order is violated and specifies that a possessor of illegal firearms who relinquishes those
firearms shall be immunized from prosecution. These definitions and clarifications will
assist the courts and law enforcement in improving the gun relinquishment process.
3. Codify the lethality factors that a judicial officer may consider for pretrial detention
when a person is charged with an intrafamily offense or violation of a civil protection
order. Currently, there is no codification alerting judicial officers, complainants, or
lawyers to the ways in which, in domestic violence situations, certain risk factors, such as
previous conviction of domestic violence or violation of protective orders and anti-stalking
orders are associated with heightened lethality. The consideration of these factors for
pretrial detention would only apply to those charged with an intrafamily offense or
violation of a civil protection order. .
4. Close a legal loophole to protect victim privacy. Currently, there is a legal loophole
identified by the Office of the Attorney General in the District’s Freedom of Information
(FOIA) laws. This provision closes that loophole by explicitly including intrafamily
offense-related body-worn camera (BWC) footage from FOIA, as well as human
trafficking-related BWC footage as assertible exemptions.
5. Add “economic or financial abuse” to the definition of domestic violence in the context
of the Domestic Violence Hotline. Federal law, in particular the Violence Against Women
Act (VAWA), understands that one manifestation of domestic and intimate partner
violence is through economic and financial abuse, understood as, “the use of or threats of
the use of force, fraud, or manipulation to coerce, impair, restrict, sabotage, interfere with
or unreasonably control an individual’s ability to acquire, use, or maintain economic
resources to which they are entitled.” Economic or financial abuse in a domestic and
intimate partner violence context already appears sporadically in the D.C. Code (e.g. § 16-
910(a)(2)(L) and § 38-952.01(2)) but remains undefined and omitted from any definitions
of domestic violence. This definition will help to expand the understanding of, and improve
responsiveness to, these aspects of domestic violence.
Should you have any questions about this legislation, please contact my Deputy Director, Elle

28 TITLE I. METROPOLITAN POLICE DEPARTMENT DOMESTIC VIOLENCE
29 PREVENTION AND RESPONSE.
30 Sec. 101. Domestic violence response.
31 (a) There is established within the Special Investigations Branch of the Metropolitan
32 Police Department (“MPD”) a Domestic Violence Response Unit (“Unit”) to coordinate
33 measures to reduce and respond to incidents of domestic violence and intimate partner violence
34 and other incidents of sexual or gender-based violence not otherwise assigned to the Sexual
35 Assault Unit, as appropriate. The Unit shall be led by the Director of Domestic Violence
36 Prevention and Intervention.
37 (b) The Unit shall, at a minimum, consist of:
38 (1) Two detectives and 2 lieutenants dedicated to pursuing domestic violence
39 warrants;
40 (2) One specialized domestic violence prosecutor, which may include a detail
41 from the United States Attorney’s Office;
42 (3) The Director of Domestic Violence Prevention and Intervention;
43 (4) One data specialist; and
44 (5) One victim and domestic violence specialist, who shall be assigned to liaise
45 with the Victim Services Branch.
2

46 (c) The Director of Domestic Violence Prevention and Intervention shall:
47 (1) Coordinate detectives and lieutenants pursuing domestic violence warrants;
48 (2) Coordinate with the District Domestic Violence Investigators and the District
49 Patrol Personnel providing initial response to incidents of domestic violence;
50 (3) Coordinate with the Domestic Violence Intake Center and MPD Detectives
51 supporting the Center;
52 (4) Directly supervise the 6 officers assigned to serve civil protection orders
53 pursuant to D.C. Official Code § 16-1007(e)(2) and develop strategies to improve service rates;
54 (5) Coordinate with the MPD training academy in the development and delivery
55 of high-quality training and best practices surrounding response and investigation of domestic
56 violence and intimate partner violence offenses, including 20 hours of training on the dynamics
57 of domestic violence, trauma resulting from domestic violence, crisis intervention, personal
58 safety, risk management, criminal and civil court processes, and resources available to victims;
59 (6) Liaise with advocacy organizations and survivors to gather feedback on
60 MPD’s services and help shape new programming;
61 (7) Work with internal and external stakeholders to enhance domestic violence
62 and intimate partner violence awareness and prevention through community outreach and
63 education, including the Office of Victim Services and Justice Grants, the advocacy
64 organization, a victim legal representation provider, a housing provider, a mental health service
65 provider, culturally specific service providers, and emergency response service providers;
66 (8) Collaborate with the Council, advocacy organizations, survivors, and service
67 providers to inform and advance legislative and policy goals aimed at preventing domestic
68 violence and intimate partner violence and strengthening intervention mechanisms;
3

69 (9) Coordinate, with support of the data specialists, the collection of data on
70 domestic violence and intimate partner violence statistics and programs, including those
71 mandated by the District, federal agencies, and other governing bodies and upon request from the
72 Criminal Justice Coordinating Council; and
73 (10) Work closely with the Office of the Mayor, the Domestic Violence Fatality
74 Review Board, and the Criminal Justice Coordinating Council.
75 (d) For the purposes of this section, the term:
76 (1) “Advocacy organization” means the federally recognized state coalition
77 against domestic violence and domestic violence programs that advocate for policy and system
78 improvements for survivors.
79 (2) “Prevention” means efforts to prevent further incidents of domestic violence,
80 including secondary or tertiary prevention.
81 (3) “Service providers” includes domestic violence programs as defined in D.C.
82 Official Code § 14–310(a)(3), including culturally specific service providers.
83 (4) “Survivors” includes survivors that are representative of DC’s diverse
84 communities and those that have and have not connected with specific resources or the criminal
85 legal system.
86 (e) Sexual assault, including sexual assault by an intimate partner, remains within the
87 MPD Sexual Assault Unit as provided in D.C. Official Code § 23-1909(d). The Unit established
88 in this section shall coordinate with the Sexual Assault Unit as appropriate.
89 TITLE II. DEFINING ECONOMIC AND FINANCIAL ABUSE.
90 Sec. 201. Section 3032 of the Domestic Violence Hotline Establishment Act of 2013,
91 effective Dec. 24, 2013 (D.C. Law 20-61; D.C. Official Code §4-551) is amended as follows:
4

92 (a) Paragraph (1) is redesignated as (1)(A).
93 (b) A new sub-paragraph (B) is added to read as follows:
94 “(B) “Economic or financial abuse,” in the context of domestic violence, dating violence,
95 and abuse in later life, means the use of or threats of the use of force, fraud, or manipulation to
96 coerce, impair, restrict, sabotage, interfere with or unreasonably control an individual’s ability to
97 acquire, use, or maintain economic resources to which they are entitled, including by:
98 “(i) Exerting undue influence or control over an individual’s financial and economic
99 behavior or decisions, including by withholding or restricting an individual’s access to, or
100 acquisition of, money, assets, credit, or financial information without their effective consent or
101 by forcing default on joint or other financial obligations, exploiting powers of attorney,
102 guardianship, or conservatorship, or failing or neglecting to act in the best interests of a person to
103 whom one has a fiduciary duty;
104 “(ii) Unfairly using the personal identifying information of another individual for one’s
105 own advantage to obtain credit, a good, a service or anything of value in the name of that
106 individual without their effective consent;
107 “(iii) Interfering with the individual’s ability to attend school or employment; or
108 “(iv) Withholding from an individual physical resources such as food, clothing, shelter,
109 medications or services necessary to maintain the individual’s physical or mental health.”.

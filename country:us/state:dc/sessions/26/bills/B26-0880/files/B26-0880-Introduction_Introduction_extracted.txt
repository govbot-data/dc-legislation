Statement of Introduction
Opioid Abatement Fund Accountability and Commission Authority Amendment Act of 2026
November 9, 2026
Today, along with Councilmembers Brooke Pinto, Matthew Frumin, Charles Allen, and Trayon
White, Sr., I am introducing the Opioid Abatement Fund Accountability and Commission
Authority Amendment Act of 2026 to address concerns about transparency, spending decisions,
and the potential replacement of existing District funding with opioid settlement dollars.
The opioid epidemic has devastated families and communities across the District. Settlement
funds represent an opportunity to expand our response, support residents living with opioid use
disorder and their families, and build the infrastructure necessary for lasting access to treatment,
harm reduction, prevention, and recovery. We have a responsibility to ensure that these resources
produce additional services and capacity and reach the communities most affected.
To date, the Office of the Attorney General has transferred approximately $38 million to the
Opioid Abatement Fund from settlements with companies whose conduct helped fuel the opioid
epidemic. Given the scale of these resources, the District must set clear spending priorities,
explain how it allocates the funds, and report on how the funds are spent and what they achieve.
The Committee has raised concerns about the Department of Behavioral Health’s proposed use
of opioid settlement funds to address budget cuts and spending pressures. We also need greater
transparency about proposed and actual allocations: what the money supports, who it serves,
what outcomes are expected, and whether it is expanding services or replacing another funding
source. Without that information, the Commission, the Council, and the public cannot adequately
assess whether these investments fulfill their purpose.
This legislation addresses those concerns by strengthening the requirement that money from the
Opioid Abatement Fund supplement existing resources. It expressly prohibits using the Fund to
fill agency operating deficits, address spending pressures, offset reductions in other funding, or
assume existing personnel and program costs that would otherwise be paid from another source.
It permits documented expansions of existing services while requiring DBH to identify the
additional capacity and costs supported by the Fund.
The bill also gives the Opioid Abatement Advisory Commission a more structured role in
guiding spending while preserving its advisory status. The Commission brings together people
with lived experience, providers, experts, and government representatives whose perspectives
should inform the District’s investments. Under this legislation, the Commission would develop
an annual proposed spending plan, with public input, identifying priorities, service gaps,
recommended investments, and expected outcomes.

DBH would be required to consider that proposal when developing its spending plan and budget
request and explain in writing which recommendations it accepts, modifies, or does not adopt.
DBH would publish proposed and final spending plans and provide an opportunity for public
comment. The bill also requires access to information and staff assistance needed for the
Commission’s work and establishes conflict-of-interest disclosure and recusal requirements.
For each proposed allocation, DBH would identify the activities to be funded, populations and
geographic areas to be served, and whether the investment creates, continues, or expands a
service. It would also disclose other funding supporting the activity and explain, with supporting
information, how the allocation supplements rather than supplants those resources.
Detailed, searchable quarterly reports would provide comparable information on actual
allocations, obligations, and expenditures, including changes from the spending plan, reductions
in other funding, and available outcomes. These requirements would allow the public to follow
spending decisions from proposal through implementation.
The legislation further requires affirmative Council approval of reprogrammings exceeding
$50,000, audits at least once every two years, and written agreements governing transfers
between District agencies. When money is improperly spent, it requires corrective action,
recovery efforts, and restoration of funds improperly used by or for a District agency.
Residents deserve confidence that opioid settlement funds are expanding access to care and
building lasting pathways to recovery. This bill establishes clearer spending restrictions and
reporting requirements so that the District can demonstrate how these resources are helping
residents and strengthening our response to the crisis.
I look forward to working with my colleagues, the Commission, the Administration, and
community partners to advance this legislation.

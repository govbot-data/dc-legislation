23 Sec. 2. (a) For the last several years, opioid overdose deaths have reached epidemic
24 proportions in the District and show no signs of slowing down. According to the most recent data
25 from the OCME, a total of 76 opioid related deaths occurred from January-May, a decrease of 25

26 percent from the same time last year. Non-fatal overdoses decreased 26 percent from the prior
27 year to date to 1,777. The epidemic continues to have a devastating impact on communities
28 across the nation, and the District of Columbia has been disproportionately affected. For the 12-
29 month period from December 1, 2024 through November 30, 2025, the District recorded more
30 than 225 fatal opioid overdoses. Black residents accounted for 84 percent of decedents, reflecting
31 a continued and disproportionate racial impact. Men comprised 64 percent of fatal overdoses,
32 while women accounted for 36 percent. Individuals ages 60 to 69 represented the largest age
33 group at 29 percent, followed by those ages 40 to 49 and 50 to 59, each at 19 percent. Non-fatal
34 overdoses occur at a substantially higher rate. For the 12-month period from February 1, 2025
35 through January 31, 2026, there were approximately 5,990 non-fatal opioid overdoses in the
36 District. Black residents accounted for 91 percent of non-fatal overdoses, compared to 5 percent
37 among White residents and 3 percent among Hispanic residents. Men comprised 77 percent of
38 individuals experiencing non-fatal overdoses. Individuals ages 40 to 49 represented the largest
39 age group at 25 percent, followed by those ages 30 to 39 and 60 to 69 at 19 percent each. Adults
40 ages 50 to 59 accounted for 17 percent of non-fatal overdoses. These data demonstrate that
41 opioid-related harm in the District remains both widespread and inequitable. Fatal overdoses
42 represent only a fraction of overdose events, and the scale of nonfatal overdoses highlights
43 significant opportunities for intervention, harm reduction, and connection to treatment. To say
44 the least, there is an ongoing public health crisis in the District. Opioid use is also associated
45 with increased risk of HIV and other infectious diseases, making outreach and engagement
46 critical components of broader public health efforts.
47 (b) To address these challenges, the DC government got proactive. On July 14, 2022,
48 Chairman Mendelson introduced the “Opioid Litigation Proceeds Amendment Act of 2022,” at
49 the request of Attorney General Karl Racine. The law was passed on December 20, 2022. The
2

50 devastating impact of the epidemic gave rise to thousands of lawsuits across the country and
51 resulted in multimillion dollar payments to local and state governments which required that at
52 least 85% be used for opioid abatement efforts. Thus, the law established the Opioid Abatement
53 Advisory Commission, erected an Office of Opioid Abatement at the Department of Behavioral
54 Health (“DBH”) to support the commission’s work, and explicitly provided that monies from the
55 settlements will be deposited into an Opioid Abatement Fund (“Fund”), a special purpose fund
56 separate from the District’s General Fund which to be managed by the agency. To be clear, § 7–
57 3221, subsection (c) states: “Money deposited into the Fund shall not be obligated or expended
58 until the Council passes legislation setting forth the permissible uses of the money in the Fund.”
59 In 2023, the Council established the Substance Abuse and Behavioral Health Services
60 Targeted Outreach Pilot Act of 2023 through the Fiscal Year 2024 Budget Support Act of 2023.
61 The pilot was designed to test the effectiveness of deploying concentrated, place-based outreach
62 efforts in high-need areas, including direct engagement, relationship-building, harm reduction
63 support, and resource brokering to connect individuals to substance use disorder treatment and
64 behavioral health services. In its first year, the pilot operated in three locations in Wards 1, 5, and
65 7. These sites were identified due to persistent open drug use, high volumes of service calls, and
66 repeated community concerns. The Ward 7 site was of particular concern, as children and infants
67 were frequently observed in the vicinity of individuals actively using drugs. Recognizing the
68 ongoing severity of the crisis, the Council expanded the pilot in the Fiscal Year 2025 Budget
69 Support Act of 2024 to include additional locations in Wards 6 and 8. Ward 8 has consistently
70 recorded the highest number of opioid-related overdose fatalities in the District since 2018. In
71 the Fiscal Year 2026 Budget Support Act of 2025, the Council directed the Department of
72 Behavioral Health to continue targeted outreach activities in six designated high need areas
73 across Wards 1, 5, 6, 7, and 8.

74 (c) The pilot program has proven to be successful. For example, one of the sites operating
75 in Ward 1 in the 500 block of T Street NW by the organization HIPS saw a 51 percent decrease
76 in nonfatal overdoses in less than a year. Even more, the pilot program was the outgrowth of the
77 successful “Community Navigators” program funded in Columbia Heights several years ago.
78 And because of the success of the community navigators and the expansion of the pilot program
79 to other wards, the Council introduced the “Place-Based Substance Use Disorder Outreach
80 Amendment Act of 2026” and passed the legislation at the May 5, 2026, Legislative Meeting.
81 The legislation codifies and makes permanent the Opioid Targeted Outreach Program (“pilot
82 program”). The Committee on Health wrote in the legislative history of the bill’s report that the
83 goal is to build on the pilot’s framework—not terminate it entirely—recognizing the need for
84 sustained, accountable, and data-driven intervention in areas most impacted by the opioid crisis.
85 The law was partially funded in 2026 and fully funded in the Fiscal Year 2027 budget by the
86 Committee on Public works. In the Fiscal Impact Statement issued by the Chief Financial Officer
87 issued on February 12, 2026, it states “DBH fiscal year 2026 budget includes $1 million in one-
88 time funding and $750,000 in recurring Local funds for outreach grants. Establishing a
89 permanent Program will require one additional employee. DBH requires $115,000 in fiscal year
90 2027 and $479,000 over the financial plan to implement the bill.” Subsequently, the Committee
91 on Public Works transferred $115,000 in recurring funds to the Committee on Health to fully
92 fund Bill 26-0226, the “Place-Based Substance Use Disorder Outreach Amendment Act of
93 2025.” (Sec. 7263. Section 3 of the Place-Based Substance Use Disorder Outreach Amendment
94 Act of 2026, effective July 18, 2026 (D.C. Law 26-142; 73 DCR 8157), is repealed.).
95 (e) In the Report and Recommendations of the Committee on Health on the Fiscal Year
96 2027 Budget for Agencies Under Its Purview, the Committee noted concerns with the direction
97 of DBH’s strategy on the opioid response particularly noting “DBH’s own testimony indicates a

98 concurrent increase in non-fatal overdoses, suggesting that while more individuals are surviving
99 overdoses, the system is not consistently succeeding in connecting those individuals to ongoing
100 treatment and recovery supports. In addition, many individuals who experience fatal overdoses
101 still have no prior engagement with treatment--reflecting persistent gaps in outreach, access, and

107 numerous hearings and roundtables over the past few years consistently emphasized that without
108 meaningful expansion of these supports, individuals will continue to cycle through emergency
109 and crisis systems rather than achieve sustained recovery.”
110 (f) In August 2026, the Department of Behavioral Health, which administers the grants
111 for the pilot program, sent out an official notification to grantee organizations operating in high
112 need areas that funding for the pilot program will conclude at the end of the current award period
113 on September 30, 2026, and will not be renewed beyond FY26. Sustained investment, stronger
114 system coordination, and a more comprehensive continuum of care is and will be necessary to
115 ensure that recent gains are preserved and translated into durable, equitable outcomes for
116 residents most impacted by the crisis.
117 (g) Based on conversations with the agency, they intend to issue new grants for Ward 7
118 and 8 using local funds from the Place-Based Substance Use Disorder Outreach Amendment Act
119 of 2026 which is fully funded for FY2027. Additionally, the agency has received $2.4 million in
120 State Opioid Response grants from the federal government to supplement local dollars in
121 targeted outreach and have issued a Request for Applications in July 2026 for opportunities in

127 (h) As the legislative history is detailed above, it was never the intent of the Council to
128 terminate the pilot program and cause a disruption in services for already identified high need
129 areas. The abrupt termination of funding for the pilot program's Fiscal Year 2026 grantees,
130 effective September 30, 2026, is inconsistent with the Council's legislative intent, as reflected in
131 the Fiscal Year 2027 Budget Support Act and the passage of the Place-Based Substance Use
132 Disorder Outreach Amendment Act of 2026, both of which contemplated a continuation and
133 expansion of outreach services rather than a lapse in services during the transition to a permanent
134 program. To prevent a disruption in services that would jeopardize the health and safety of
135 residents in high-need areas and undermine the progress achieved under the pilot program, the
136 Department of Behavioral Health is directed and authorized by this body to continue funding all
137 current Fiscal Year 2026 grantee organizations at their existing locations, using funds from the
138 Opioid Abatement Fund, until such time as a permanent program is formally established and
139 commenced pursuant to the Place-Based Substance Use Disorder Outreach Amendment Act of
140 2026.

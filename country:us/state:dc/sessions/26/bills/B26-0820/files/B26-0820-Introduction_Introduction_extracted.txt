Dear Secretary Howard,
District residents deserve access to safe, affordable homes in every Ward. Yet today, many of our most
urgently needed housing projects face regulatory processes that add cost, delay, and uncertainty that have
led to historic lows in new construction.1 Recent analyses of DC’s housing ecosystem show that costs
have dramatically increased due in part to elongated permitting timelines, duplicative reviews, and
regulatory requirements that do not always scale appropriately.2
To help address these challenges, I am introducing the Build Housing Package, a set of four targeted bills
designed to lower costs, speed project delivery, and make our limited housing dollars go further. These
reforms are practical, specific, and grounded in findings from developers, nonprofit housing providers,
agency personnel, and technical experts. The package is endorsed by Greater Greater Washington and the
Pew Charitable Trusts, was drafted in collaboration with The Coalition, DC Policy Center, and the
Department of Buildings, and incorporates feedback from the Housing Unity permitting reform working
group convened by the Federal City Council.
1. Understanding the Cost of Housing Regulations Act of 2026
The District currently has no insight into the cumulative or individual cost burden of its housing
regulations. Requirements are often adopted individually without assessing how they interact when
stacked on a single project. The result is a regulatory environment where individually reasonable rules can
collectively produce outsized costs and delays, undermining project feasibility and reducing the number
of units our subsidy dollars can support.3 This bill establishes a third-party cost review comparing DC’s
development costs, permitting timelines, and regulatory burdens with Maryland and Virginia peer
jurisdictions to equip the District to understand the tradeoffs in its current regulatory environment.

2. Affordable Housing Tax Assessment Alignment Act of 2026
Affordable housing should not be taxed as though it can collect market rate rents. Yet that is what
happened at Intersect, a 74-unit mixed-income development at 1338 8th Street NW with 24 affordable
units. The Office of Tax and Revenue (OTR) assessed those affordable units at $5.75 million, even though
Roadside Development's analysis found that their actual operating income supported a value of negative
$630,000 before taxes—a difference of roughly $6.38 million.
The owner appealed its Tax Year 2025 assessment of $39.97 million and ultimately obtained a court-
ordered reduction to $34.5 million—a reduction of approximately $5.47 million. But the correction took
nearly two years. The owner had to pay the full, unreduced tax bill while the appeal was pending, and
after finally securing the reduction, 25% of the tax savings went to the tax consultant.
Intersect was developed through a public-private partnership, making the disconnect particularly
concerning: the District had already imposed the affordability requirements and had information about the
property's legally restricted rents. Yet that information did not make its way to OTR in a way that allowed
the affordable units to be accurately assessed.
This problem persists across all affordable housing development and is even greater for fully affordable
developments. A mixed-income project can use revenue from market-rate units to help absorb the costs
of affordable units, but inflating tax assessments on fully affordable developments reduces the resources
available to operate and preserve affordable homes.
This bill closes that information gap by requiring the Department of Housing and Community
Development (DHCD) to provide OTR with the affordability covenant data it needs to account for legally
restricted rents when assessing property value. The District should not require properties to remain
affordable and then tax them as though they are not. Councilmember Pinto and I are co-leading this bill.
Chairman Mendelson and Councilmembers Bonds, Allen, Nadeau, and Frumin have co-introduced.
3. Pre-Approved Housing Plans Act of 2026
Small-scale housing projects should not have to pay for the same design and technical work every time a
builder wants to construct a rowhouse, townhouse, duplex, or other small multifamily building. This bill
establishes a pre-approved housing plan program within the Department of Buildings (DOB) to make
permit-ready, code-compliant plans publicly available for these repeatable housing types. DOB would
develop or competitively procure standardized plans, while also allowing previously approved plans to
be reused within three years, and would publish the zoning, construction, and site requirements
associated with each plan. The plans would be designed for adaptation to different lots and site
conditions without requiring a complete redesign, creating a more predictable and affordable pathway
for small-scale housing development.
This approach has been adopted by jurisdictions across the country. A 2026 report from The Pew
Charitable Trusts found that roughly 40 jurisdictions have implemented some form of pre-approved

building plan program and estimates that these programs can reduce overall development costs. Pre-
approved plans can reduce architectural and engineering costs and shorten permitting timelines by
completing much of the technical review upfront. Pew also found that these programs can be particularly
valuable for smaller and newer builders, for whom upfront design and permitting costs can make small
infill and “missing middle” projects difficult to finance. The District can build on this model while
tailoring it to the housing types and development conditions that are most appropriate for DC.
Councilmember Pinto and I are co-leading this bill. Councilmembers Bonds, Allen, Nadeau, Lewis
George, and Frumin, have co-introduced.
4. Risk-Based Third-Party Code Review Act of 2026
Today’s statutory third-party review system does not shorten permitting timelines. Even when applicants
pay qualified external architects or engineers to certify plans, DOB still performs a full technical review,
meaning third-party review adds a step without replacing one. Applicants pay twice, and the government
queue remains unchanged.
This bill establishes a binding, risk-based third-party pathway, under which DOB would rely on certified
submissions for defined project types unless the agency identifies a specific defect within a set review
window. DOB would shift to targeted auditing rather than universal re-review, with oversight scaled based
on project complexity and the performance history of the development team. This approach adds usable
private-sector review capacity, frees DOB staff for complex, high-risk work, prevents costly
construction-stage delays through timely inspections, and maintains safety via qualifications, standardized
certifications, audits, and meaningful sanctions.
DOB would issue implementing rules on eligibility, qualifications, audit rates, defect thresholds, and
enforcement. This bill is co-introduced by Councilmember Pinto.
These four bills target avoidable cost drivers, create predictable pathways for small and routine projects,
align tax policy with affordability commitments, and provide the District with the tools needed to respond
to changing market conditions. Together, they strengthen our ability to deliver housing efficiently and
responsibly, ensuring that public investments translate into real units for District residents.
I look forward to working with the Council to advance these reforms. Please contact the Committee on
Housing staff at housing@dccouncil.gov with any questions.

Dear Secretary Smith,
Today, I am introducing the “Events DC Excess Cash Calculation Clarification Amendment
Act of 2026.” to clarify excess cash calculation requirements outlined in existing law. Please find
enclosed a signed copy of the legislation.
On October 8, 2025, the Office of the District of Columbia Auditor (“ODCA”) published a
report entitled, “Events DC Wrongly Calculated “Excess Cash,” Owes D.C. $68.7 Million”. The purpose of
this audit was to determine whether Events DC’s excess cash transfer calculations complied with
governing laws and agreements.1 According to DC Code § 10-1202.13, the Washington Convention
and Sports Authority, also known as Events DC, is required to transfer excess cash to the District of
Columbia government at the end of each fiscal year.
ODCA and Events DC disagree on the calculation of Events DC’s excess cash for FY 2024.
In its report, ODCA found that Events DC incorrectly calculated its excess cash and contends that
Events DC owes the District $68.7 million. Events DC disagreed with the ODCA’s finding, noting
in their response that Events DC had reviewed this issue with staff, the Office of the Chief Financial
Officer, their independent third-party auditors, and outside bond counsel, and asserts that their
excess cash calculation is consistent with their bond agreements and longstanding prior practice.
Events DC further noted in its response to the ODCA report that their excess cash
calculation had already undergone an in-depth review and audit back in 2019, which arose from the
fiscal sufficiency of the Fiscal Year 2020 Budget. On June 10, 2019, former Chief Financial Officer
Jeffrey DeWitt provided a letter to the DC Council outlining an error in the excess cash
calculations.2 Consequently, Events DC made the correction and revised the excess cash calculations

Moreover, Events DC issues bonds. In an existing agreement, the Amended and Restated
Master Trust Agreement dated as of February 1, 2007 (“MTA”), provisions were in place to set
protections for bondholders, including an outline of necessary funds and reserves. Events DC noted
that calculating excess cash in the manner ODCA set forth would eliminate Events DC’s ability to
fulfill its operational responsibilities and breach the covenants in existing agreements with
bondholders. In former Chief Financial Officer DeWitt’s June 10, 2019, letter, he explains that an
intentional breach of the MTA could trigger lawsuits by bondholders and impact the District’s credit
rating. Such a breach could increase the costs of borrowing and impair Events DC’s ability to issue
debt.
Events DC has multiple operating funds including a Convention Center Fund, Sports and
Entertainment Fund and Marketing Fund. Events DC’s Sports and Entertainment Division and its
Marketing Fund rely on tax revenues to keep running and cannot operate independently of
dedicated tax revenues in the Convention Center Fund. If those expenses are left out of the
calculation, as ODCA recommends, the District could send excess cash to the General Fund, but
Events DC would still need the same funding to operate. Consequently, the District would then
have to give Events DC a new subsidy from the budget. This would create an unnecessary funding
issue, which was not the intent of §10-1202.13.
Evidently, the excess cash calculation outlined by §10-1202.13 allows for multiple
interpretations, such that further clarification is necessary. Both ODCA and Events DC concur that
legislative clarity is the most efficient way to resolve ambiguity. The purpose of this legislation is to
provide technical clarifications to ensure the calculation is conducted consistently with the MTA,
which will prevent risks of breaches, potential bondholder litigation, and financial exposure
negatively impacting the District’s/Events DC’s credit rating and borrowing costs. Additionally, the
technical clarifications ensure the statutory requirements reflect Events DC’s expansion of scope
and remain aligned with current operations.
